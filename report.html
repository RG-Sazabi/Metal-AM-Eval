Metal AM Decision Support Tools: Cost and Benefit Coverage

Updated October 29, 2026 · 224 tools reviewed

Summary

We found 224 decision support tools for metal AM adoption published since 2010, and none prices all eight cost and benefit columns. They include 36 commercial products, 75 academic models, 24 government or consortium tools, and 89 methods, frameworks and standards; 108 come from defense. The broadest tools rate Yes on five columns, while 134 tools rate Yes on none. Another 490 of the 1,792 ratings (27%) are Unknown, because public sources can't confirm them either way.

Coverage splits along the cost–benefit line. Production cost is best covered (56 Yes), while QA qualification (5), shared capacity (7) and NRE (9) are worst. Cost models price production; supply-chain models value storage, speed and shared capacity but treat AM cost as a lump sum. Only three tools (Busachi, Coyle and Braunberger) rate Yes on at least two costs and two benefits, and no qualification Yes pairs with a benefit Yes.

For defense sustainment, the best partial fits are the Cranfield/Babcock Royal Navy framework (Busachi et al.) and Coyle's NPS naval aviation model (Coyle). AFIT's laser powder bed cost models (Braunberger; Crean) add Air Force cost data, and the NCSU/USMA network model (Doudnikoff, extending McDermott et al.) values the benefits. For QA, only the Peron/Cantini model prices qualification by part class; NASA-STD-6030 and NAVSEA rules list what to price.

Fielded defense tools are mostly part screens. Commercial screeners now serve Navy submarine spares (CASTOR) and the Marine Corps and Army (3YOURMIND). DLA's prescreener, the Navy's AMCAT and the Air Force's PACT are government-built; AMCAT and PACT are known mainly from press releases, so their ratings carry low confidence.

The NIAR tool. The only NIAR decision tool found is the AM Techno-Economic Analysis (TEA) cost model and module, America Makes IMPACT project 5554.007 (2023–2025). OSD ManTech funded it through AFRL, so it is DoD-wide rather than Air Force-specific; AFLCMC is the only Air Force member of its advisory board. It scores highest among government tools, but mostly Partial: the January 2024 interim briefing reviews existing cost models and lists what a tool still to be built would cover. No final report was public by October 2026.

NIAR's other Air Force work, such as the WAM materials database and B-1 and F-16 digital twins, is not decision support. PACT belongs to the Rapid Sustainment Office. The TEA briefing lists a Christophe Combemale on the team; a CMU researcher shares that name, but the briefing gives no affiliation.

Scope and scoring method

Each tool is scored Yes, Partial or No on eight cost and benefit columns, with a note and a source for every rating.

What counts as a tool. Commercial software, academic models, government and consortium tools, and methods or frameworks with no software (checklists, risk classification schemes, standards that drive decisions).

Lens. Defense sustainment: spare parts, maintenance depots and readiness. Civilian sustainment work (rail, energy, maritime, aviation repair and overhaul) is included when it applies.

Bounds. Published or released 2010 onward. All metal processes: powder bed fusion, directed energy deposition, binder jetting, cold spray and wire-arc. Four decision levels: which parts to print, which process or machine, whether to adopt or buy, and where to place and share capacity.

Column What it covers
NRE One-time cost to prepare design files and set run parameters: reverse engineering, redesign, build prep, parameter development
Production Per-part cost from geometry, material and machine
QA: qualification Material, process and part qualification; first-article testing; airworthiness or technical-authority approval
QA: inspection Per-part inspection and testing: NDI or CT, witness coupons, mechanical tests
Storage Inventory reduction because spares can be printed on demand
Shared capital Machine purchase and maintenance cost spread across many parts or users
Shared capacity Print capacity pooled across sites or units, which changes lead time
Speed Dollarized or quantified value of having parts when needed instead of waiting weeks to months

Ratings. Yes means the tool models or quantifies the item as an input, calculation or output. Partial means it is treated qualitatively (a screening criterion, a flag, a crude proxy) or only part of the column is covered. No means a full description of the tool was read, or its documented scope plainly excludes the item. Unknown means public sources can't confirm it either way, for example when only a press release or an abstract is available. Tools with thin documentation are marked low confidence.

Checking. An independent reviewer re-checked 133 of the highest-stakes ratings against their sources and changed 22, mostly Yes to Partial; the corrections are applied throughout. Shared capacity counts as Yes when a model has parts or users contend for shared printer capacity that sets lead time, even at one site; the notes say when no cross-site pooling is modeled.

Second pass. Every rating was then re-checked against what its sources actually show, which moved 490 ratings to Unknown. Each tool also carries a direct link to the tool itself (product page, paper, thesis, standard or portal) where one is public; 190 of 224 do. Where a tool was released more than once, only the newest release is listed and earlier ones are named in its entry: Khajavi's 2014 F/A-18 model is folded into its 2018 follow-on, and McDermott et al.'s network model into Doudnikoff's 2021 extension.

Commonalities

Most tools follow the same chain (screen parts, cost them, build a business case), but most cover only one or two links. Screens use nearly the same inputs: material, build envelope, unit price, demand, lead time and sometimes criticality (DLA; 3YOURMIND; CASTOR).

Screens also pass similarly small shares of parts. DLA's prescreener cut 4.5 million parts to about 40,000, then to 40 (Additive Manufacturing Media). Coyle cut 10,162 Navy aviation items to 5, and VTT found about 2% of one firm's items feasible (Coyle; DIVA).

Coverage across all 224 tools is uneven, and academic models hold most Yes ratings in every column except QA qualification.

Commercial screening and cost tools are production-cost engines. Of 36 products, 13 rate Yes on production, and none on NRE, QA qualification or shared capacity. 3YOURMIND Part Identification alone itemizes stock and downtime avoidance; AMPOWER and ExOne's calculator alone make capital use explicit.

Academic part-cost models add shared capital through build packing. Baumers et al. found that single-geometry builds overstate unit cost by a mean of 157% (Baumers et al.). Lindemann's lifecycle model also prices design work, but few others touch NRE, QA or any benefit (Lindemann et al.).

Supply-chain and inventory models carry the benefit columns. Academic models, mostly of this kind, hold 18 of 21 storage, 23 of 29 speed and all 7 shared-capacity Yes ratings. They agree that some stock should remain (Song and Zhang; Knofius et al.) and that centralizing wins when machine capital is high (Khajavi et al.; McDermott et al.).

QA standards classify parts by criticality and attach activities to each class. NASA-STD-6030, USAF EZ-SB-19-01 and NAVSEA's framework set test data and inspection scope by class but give no prices (NASA; USAF; NAVSEA). Most let one-time qualification cover many parts, as in NAVSEA's once-per-process/material-combination rule (Hovanec).

Government and consortium tools mostly rate Partial, with no Yes among 24 on qualification, storage, shared capital or shared capacity. Only AMCAT prices NRE (AMCAT). Their screening criteria echo commercial tools, which the services now buy for the same job (Army).

Gaps

The costs and benefits that weigh most in defense sustainment are the ones tools rarely dollarize: qualification, NRE, shared capacity and readiness value.

QA qualification. Only 5 of 224 tools rate Yes, and four are rule sets that fix qualification activities by part class without prices: NASA-STD-6030, NAVSEA's framework, DNV-ST-B203 and IRClass's 2025 notes. The Peron/Cantini model is the only costed entry; Lawand et al. include only a fixed certificate fee.

Peron/Cantini shows qualification can decide spare-part economics. Adding DNV class-based testing cut AM's break-even quantity from 42 parts to 8 for class C2 and 6 for class C3 (Peron et al.). Its reported qualification share recomputes lower from its own inputs (about 62% versus 90%), so use its shares with care.

Elsewhere, qualification costs appear only as anchors, not models:

  • MMPDS sets an S-basis floor of 30 samples across 3 heats, lots, machines and builds, and recommends 100 for C- or D-basis allowables (Hall, MMPDS).
  • JMADD spent $4.24M over four years on a public Ti-6Al-4V allowables program with about 1,733 first-phase specimens (JMADD). By our division that is roughly $2,400 per specimen, though the budget covered more than those tests.
  • APL says NAVSEA machine certification once needed 300 test samples and millions of dollars, a burden it helped cut by over 60% (JHU APL).
  • An FAA briefing puts the entry cost for critical AM in the tens of millions of dollars, against about $1M for equipment (Gorelik). For low-criticality parts the bar is much lower: FRCE certified its first non-flight-critical metal parts in under six months (Navy.mil).

QA per-part inspection. Inspection fares a little better, with 12 Yes ratings, but CT is rarely priced. Strutner et al. estimate about $1,480 per CT specimen in-house and $2,300 outsourced, with analysis labor the largest share (Strutner et al.). In a laser powder bed case, Verna et al. found quality costs of €14.40–31.69 per part against €18.56 for production (Verna et al.).

NRE. Only 9 tools model NRE; 52 confirmably leave it out and 71 don't document it either way, though legacy defense parts often lack the data needed to print them. About 75% of DLA-managed parts lack technical data packages (Langlais et al.), and Crean cites AFLCMC's figure of about $1M to complete the NRE for one part (Crean). Braunberger reports reverse engineering often takes 150+ hours and parameter testing can pass $150,000 (Braunberger).

Shared capacity. This is the thinnest benefit: only 7 tools rate Yes, all academic, and three of them model one shared site rather than a network. None combines build batching, post-processing bottlenecks such as HIP, and qualification lead times (McDermott et al.). FAA AC 33.15-3 and AIA guidance qualify each machine separately, so pooling capacity does not pool qualification (FAA; AIA).

Value of speed. Speed has 29 Yes ratings, but most measure lead time, availability or a generic backorder penalty. Metal AM often loses on unit price and wins on time: six C-130 parts averaged 27.6% above DLA prices but arrived in 9–16 days versus 71 to 120+ (Braunberger). Khajavi values aircraft downtime at about $255 per hour, a depreciation proxy that likely understates readiness value (Khajavi et al.), and no model prices lead time per not-mission-capable aircraft-day.

Polymer cases and thin documentation. Several widely cited defense cases are polymer-only, including Khajavi's F/A-18 ducts, Daugherty and Heiple's Marine Corps study, and a Bundeswehr simulation; their value logic transfers to metal, but their cost data do not. Ratings that public sources can't confirm are marked Unknown rather than No: 27% of all ratings, 36% for commercial products and 40% for government tools such as PACT, AMCAT, AMIGA, SEEKR and NIAR's TEA, which are known only from press releases or briefings.

Integration framework

Existing tools can be chained into a five-layer pipeline that passes each part's data, criticality class and costs forward; three missing modules would complete it. Every layer keys on the part number (NIIN), and the criticality class set at screening drives QA cost later. One-time costs (NRE, qualification, machines) stay separate from recurring costs, so the portfolio layer can spread them across parts.

  1. Part screening → ranked candidates, likely process, criticality class. Inputs: supply records (NIIN, demand, unit price, lead times, backorders, criticality code, technical data package status), drawings or CAD, and machine–material data. Tools: the DLA prescreener, 3YOURMIND or CASTOR, Knofius's weighted ranking, and NASA's AM-risk checklist. Missing: a standard map from supply criticality codes to QA classes, and a data-package flag that routes parts to NRE costing.
  2. Per-part cost → one-time NRE, plus unit cost and lead time by batch size. Inputs: candidates, machine rates, labor and post-processing steps. Tools: NIAR's planned four-process TEA model, the Braunberger and Crean models, Lindemann's activity-based model and AMPOWER, with NRE elements from Busachi and AMCAT. Missing: a reverse-engineering and data-package cost model; AFLCMC's $1M and Braunberger's 150 hours are only anchors.
  3. QA cost by criticality class → one-time qualification per machine–material pair, plus per-part inspection. Inputs: the class from ASTM F3572, NAVSEA severity levels or USAF structural categories. Tools: activity bundles from NASA-STD-6030, EZ-SB-19-01, NAVSEA tech pubs and MMPDS, priced with the Peron/Cantini structure and JMADD, Strutner and Verna cost data. Missing: priced bundles per class, and the cost of proving machine equivalence across sites (Delta Qual).
  4. Network and inventory model → storage savings, machines and sites, lead time under load, availability. Inputs: demand distributions, holding cost, backorder penalty, machine capacity, and the costs from layers 2 and 3. Tools: NCSU/USMA network models (Doudnikoff), Song and Zhang's printer queue, Cokyasar and Jin's capacity allocation, Westerweel's remote-site model, Knofius's dual sourcing, and Bonnín Roca's site model. Missing: HIP, machining and inspection bottlenecks, per-machine qualification, and a dollar value per not-mission-capable day.
  5. Portfolio business case → which parts, processes, sites and qualifications to fund. Inputs: per-part results from layer 4, plus budgets. Tools: Busachi's decision support system, Heinen and Hoberg's portfolio switch-over logic, Braunberger's multi-objective scoring for non-dollar criteria, and NIAR's planned TEA module. Missing: an optimizer that spreads qualification and capital across services, with net present value and readiness outputs.

Feedback loops matter as much as the forward chain. Machine use from layer 4 changes unit cost in layer 2; build packing alone can shift it more than 2.5 times (Baumers et al.). The portfolio chosen in layer 5 sets how many parts share each qualified process, and so the per-part qualification cost in layer 3.

The three missing modules are an NRE cost model for legacy parts, priced QA bundles by criticality class, and a readiness value per not-mission-capable day. NIAR's TEA and CMU's Bonnín Roca model, which links process cost to site choice, are natural starting points (Bonnín Roca et al.).
