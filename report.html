Metal AM Decision Support Tools: Cost and Benefit Coverage

Updated October 29, 2026 · 215 tools reviewed

Summary

We reviewed 224 decision support tools for metal AM adoption published since 2010 and kept 215; none prices all eight cost and benefit columns. They include 35 commercial products, 69 academic models, 24 government or consortium tools, and 87 methods, frameworks and standards; 101 come from defense. By type, 138 are tools that compute an answer or are used hands-on, and 77 are frameworks for thinking about the problem. For 153 tools a source shows metal AM is in scope; the other 62 are AM tools whose sources never say whether they cover metal, and they are flagged "metal not stated". We dropped 9: eight polymer-only studies and one general-purpose cost estimator. The broadest tools rate Yes on five columns, while 131 rate Yes on none, and 593 of the 1,720 ratings (34%) are Unknown because public sources can't confirm them either way.

Coverage splits along the cost–benefit line. Production cost is best covered (56 Yes), while shared capacity (5), qualification (6) and NRE (7) are worst. Partial means the tool prices part of an item or uses it as a criterion in its decision; a tool that only mentions an item or claims a benefit rates No or Unknown, so 366 of the 1,720 ratings are Partial. Cost models price production; supply-chain models value storage and speed but treat AM cost as a lump sum. Only one tool, Knofius et al.'s dual-sourcing model, rates Yes on at least two costs and two benefits, and no qualification Yes pairs with a benefit Yes.

For defense sustainment, the broadest fits remain the Cranfield/Babcock Royal Navy framework (Busachi et al.) and AFIT's laser powder bed cost models (Crean; Braunberger), alongside Coyle's NPS naval aviation model (Coyle); after the adversarial review none of them rates Yes on two costs and two benefits. The benefit side comes from network and inventory models: Jedeck's spare-parts simulation and the NCSU/USMA network model (Doudnikoff, extending McDermott et al.) both rate Yes on storage, shared capital, shared capacity and speed, though Doudnikoff's sources don't say whether it covers metal. For QA, the Peron/Cantini model prices qualification by part class and NASA-STD-6030 sets costable tests for every class; NAVSEA's framework decides whether qualification applies but leaves the tests to each part.

Fielded defense tools are mostly part screens. Commercial screeners now serve Navy submarine spares (CASTOR) and the Marine Corps and Army (3YOURMIND). DLA's prescreener and the Air Force's PACT are government-built; PACT is known mainly from press releases, so its ratings carry low confidence. The Navy Price Fighters' AMCAT cost and time model, readmitted in this round, is the only government tool with a Yes (it costs NRE explicitly), and NAVSEA is wiring it into technical data package development.

The NIAR tool. The only NIAR decision tool found is the AM Techno-Economic Analysis (TEA) cost model and module, America Makes IMPACT project 5554.007 (2023–2025). OSD ManTech funded it through AFRL, so it is DoD-wide rather than Air Force-specific; AFLCMC is the only Air Force member of its advisory board. All eight of its ratings are Unknown: the January 2024 interim briefing reviews existing cost models and lists what a tool still to be built would cover, so there is no method yet to rate. No final report was public by October 2026.

NIAR's other Air Force work, such as the WAM materials database and B-1 and F-16 digital twins, is not decision support. PACT belongs to the Rapid Sustainment Office. The TEA briefing lists a Christophe Combemale on the team; a CMU researcher shares that name, but the briefing gives no affiliation.

Scope and scoring method

Each tool is rated on eight cost and benefit columns, with a note and a source for every rating; the rating rules are below.

What counts as a tool. Commercial software, academic models, government and consortium tools, and methods or frameworks with no software (checklists, risk classification schemes, standards that drive decisions). Each entry is typed as a Tool or a Framework. Tools compute an answer or are hands-on aids: software, spreadsheets, calculators, databases, quantitative models and worksheets. Frameworks are ways to think about the problem: qualitative methods, standards, guidance and policy.

Lens. Defense sustainment: spare parts, maintenance depots and readiness. Civilian sustainment work (rail, energy, maritime, aviation repair and overhaul) is included when it applies.

Bounds. Published or released 2010 onward. All metal processes: powder bed fusion, directed energy deposition, binder jetting, cold spray and wire-arc. A tool counts if a source shows metal AM is in scope (it models, screens for or supports metal processes or materials, alongside polymer or not, was applied to metal parts, or is used inside a named program that prints metal parts), or if it is an AM tool whose sources never say whether it covers metal; those 62 are flagged "metal not stated" in the matrix, which can filter them out. Polymer-only studies and general-purpose tools where AM is incidental are excluded: 9 of the 224 tools reviewed were dropped on these grounds. Four decision levels: which parts to print, which process or machine, whether to adopt or buy, and where to place and share capacity.

Rating rules

Every rating describes what the tool itself does (its method, model, software or prescribed procedure), as shown by a source, and carries a one-sentence note naming that source. There are four ratings:

  • Yes: the item is an explicit input, calculation or output that meets the column's test below. On the qualification and inspection columns, a standard also counts as Yes when it prescribes costable activities by part class.
  • Partial: the tool takes the item into its decision without meeting the Yes test. Either its method puts numbers on part of the column, or the item is a screening criterion or threshold, a yes/no gate, a scored factor, or a step its procedure requires. A mention, a claimed or case-study saving, a planned feature, or a service the tool performs without weighing its cost does not count.
  • No: confirmed not addressed, because a full description of the tool was read and the item is absent or only mentioned, or the tool's scope plainly excludes it.
  • Unknown: public sources can't settle it, for example when only an abstract or a press release is available, or when such a source mentions the item without showing the tool uses it.

The coverage score counts Yes as 1 and Partial as 0.5, out of 8. Tools with thin documentation are marked low confidence.

Two lines are judgment calls. Speed counts as Yes without a dollar value, and shared capacity counts competition for printers at a single site; the notes say when no cross-site pooling is modeled.

Checking

Ratings were checked five times. An independent reviewer first re-checked 133 of the highest-stakes ratings and changed 22, mostly Yes to Partial, and a second pass moved ratings the sources could not confirm to Unknown. In early November 2026, every rating for the then 144 metal-scope tools was re-checked under the rules above (170 of 1,152 changed), and a separate auditor confirmed 55 of 60 sampled ratings.

The 80 tools cut in an earlier metal-only pass were then readmitted, and all 224 went through an adversarial review. In batches of 15 to 17 tools, an advocate agent re-rated each tool from its sources with a quote or locator for every rating; a skeptic agent independently tried to overturn every rating in both directions, opening the source behind every Yes, every changed rating and every metal-scope call; and a judge agent ruled on each dispute from the sources. Skeptics contested 121 of the 1,792 ratings and 10 metal-scope calls. Judges sided with the skeptic on 97 ratings and 7 metal calls and with the advocate on 21 ratings and 2 metal calls, and chose a third rating 3 times. In all, 179 ratings changed: 106 rose from No or Unknown to Partial, 25 rose to Yes and 15 fell from Yes, and Unknowns fell by 53 net. Nine tools were dropped: eight polymer-only studies (among them Khajavi's 2018 spare-parts network and Westerweel's field-printing model) and Galorath's general-purpose SEER estimator. One drop overrides a judge: Daugherty and Heiple's Marine Corps study was kept on a program-level metal link, but its own analysis covers only polymer printers.

One Partial. The survey first split Partial into quantified and qualitative kinds, and the qualitative kind also credited mentions and claims. To stop those counting as coverage, the two kinds were merged under the stricter test above. Two reviewer agents independently re-ruled each of the 619 former qualitative Partials from its note, opening the source where the note was unclear; they agreed on 578, and a judge settled the other 41 and one flagged cell from the sources. In all, 185 stayed Partial, 202 became No and 232 became Unknown, so Unknown rose from 21% to 34% of ratings; no Yes rating changed.

Tool links and versions. Each tool carries a direct link to the tool itself (product page, paper, thesis, standard or portal) where one is public; 179 of 215 do, and every link was re-checked in November 2026. Where a tool was released more than once, only the newest release is listed and earlier ones are named in its entry; McDermott et al.'s network model, for example, is folded into Doudnikoff's 2021 extension.

Commonalities

Most tools follow the same chain (screen parts, cost them, build a business case), but most cover only one or two links. Screens use nearly the same inputs: material, build envelope, unit price, demand, lead time and sometimes criticality (DLA; 3YOURMIND; CASTOR).

Screens also pass similarly small shares of parts. DLA's prescreener cut 4.5 million parts to about 40,000, then to 40 (Additive Manufacturing Media). Coyle cut 10,162 Navy aviation items to 5, and VTT found about 2% of one firm's items feasible (Coyle; DIVA).

Coverage across all 215 tools is uneven, and academic models hold most Yes ratings in every column except qualification.

Commercial screening and cost tools are production-cost engines. Of 35 products, 12 rate Yes on production, and none on NRE, qualification or shared capacity. 3YOURMIND Part Identification alone itemizes stock; AMPOWER, ExOne's calculator, aPriori and 3D Spark make capital use explicit, and six products compute a lead time or delivery estimate.

Academic part-cost models add shared capital through build packing. Baumers et al. found that single-geometry builds overstate unit cost by a mean of 157% (Baumers et al.). Lindemann's lifecycle model lists design work but costs only per-build job preparation, and few others price NRE, QA or any benefit (Lindemann et al.).

Supply-chain and inventory models carry the benefit columns. Academic models, mostly of this kind, hold 17 of the 20 storage Yes ratings, 20 of the 28 speed Yes ratings and all 5 shared-capacity Yes ratings. AM cuts stock most when holding costs are high and failure rates are well known: an aircraft spares model keeps AM inventory at about a quarter of conventional levels (Ghadge et al.), while uncertainty about AM failure rates erodes the advantage (Peron et al. 2024). On placement, centralizing wins when machine and post-processing capital is high (McDermott et al.; Bonnín Roca et al.), and Doudnikoff finds no AM cheapest once machine fixed cost is counted (Doudnikoff).

QA standards classify parts by criticality and attach activities to each class. NASA-STD-6030 sets test data and inspection scope for every class but gives no prices; USAF EZ-SB-19-01 and NAVSEA's framework classify parts by criticality but leave much of the testing to each part (NASA; USAF; NAVSEA). Most let one-time qualification cover many parts, as in NAVSEA's once-per-process/material-combination rule (Hovanec).

Government and consortium tools mostly rate Unknown (65% of their ratings), and only one of the 24 earns a Yes: the Navy's AMCAT, which costs NRE. Singapore's maritime screening programme, once rated Yes on production, turned out on re-reading to quote savings from partner cases rather than compute them. Their screening criteria echo commercial tools, which the services now buy for the same job (Army).

Gaps

The costs and benefits that weigh most in defense sustainment are the ones tools rarely dollarize: qualification, NRE, shared capacity and readiness value.

Qualification. Only 6 of 215 tools rate Yes. Three are rule sets that fix qualification activities by part class without prices: NASA-STD-6030, DNV-ST-B203 and IRClass's 2025 notes. NAVSEA's framework, rated Yes before the adversarial review, uses severity levels to decide whether qualification applies but leaves the tests to each part. Three put a cost on it: the Peron/Cantini model prices test sets by part class, a Leonardo aircraft-support model carries qualification as a cost term with its values withheld (Viano), and Lynch et al. minimize the cost of a requalification test campaign (Lynch et al.). Lawand et al. include only a fixed certificate fee.

Peron/Cantini shows qualification can decide spare-part economics. Adding DNV class-based testing cut AM's break-even quantity from 42 parts to 8 for class C2 and 6 for class C3 (Peron et al.). Its reported qualification share recomputes lower from its own inputs (about 62% versus 90%), so use its shares with care.

Elsewhere, qualification costs appear only as anchors, not models:

  • MMPDS sets an S-basis floor of 30 samples across 3 heats, lots, machines and builds, and recommends 100 for C- or D-basis allowables (Hall, MMPDS).
  • JMADD spent $4.24M over four years on a public Ti-6Al-4V allowables program with about 1,733 first-phase specimens (JMADD). By our division that is roughly $2,400 per specimen, though the budget covered more than those tests.
  • APL says NAVSEA machine certification once needed 300 test samples and millions of dollars, a burden it helped cut by over 60% (JHU APL).
  • An FAA briefing puts the entry cost for critical AM in the tens of millions of dollars, against about $1M for equipment (Gorelik). For low-criticality parts the bar is much lower: FRCE certified its first non-flight-critical metal parts in under six months (Navy.mil).

QA per-part inspection. Inspection fares a little better, with 11 Yes ratings, but CT is rarely priced. Strutner et al. estimate about $1,480 per CT specimen in-house and $2,300 outsourced, with analysis labor the largest share (Strutner et al.). In a laser powder bed case, Verna et al. found quality costs of €14.40–31.69 per part against €18.56 for production (Verna et al.).

NRE. Only 7 tools put NRE in a cost model and 54 touch it only partly; 58 confirmably leave it out and 96 don't document it either way, though legacy defense parts often lack the data needed to print them. About 75% of DLA-managed parts lack technical data packages (Langlais et al.), and Crean cites AFLCMC's figure of about $1M to complete the NRE for one part (Crean). Braunberger reports reverse engineering often takes 150+ hours and parameter testing can pass $150,000 (Braunberger).

Shared capacity. This is the thinnest benefit: only five tools rate Yes, all academic models (Doudnikoff's network model, Jedeck's and O'Brien's simulations, Song and Zhang's shared-printer queue, and Cokyasar and Jin's capacity allocation), and 120 of 215 confirmably leave it out. None combines build batching, post-processing bottlenecks such as HIP, and qualification lead times (McDermott et al.). FAA AC 33.15-3 and AIA guidance qualify each machine separately, so pooling capacity does not pool qualification (FAA; AIA).

Value of speed. Speed has 28 Yes ratings, but most measure lead time, availability or a generic backorder penalty. Metal AM often loses on unit price and wins on time: six C-130 parts averaged 27.6% above DLA prices but arrived in 9–16 days versus 71 to 120+ (Braunberger). Coyle's Navy aviation case shows the same pattern: $3,145 in about 10 days by AM against $7,005 in 545 days from the OEM (Coyle). No model prices lead time per not-mission-capable aircraft-day.

Excluded cases and thin documentation. Polymer-only studies are left out even when widely cited: Khajavi's spare-parts network, Daugherty and Heiple's Marine Corps study, and the Einhorn, Westerweel (field printing) and Morgan and Prentiss defense models print only in plastic. Their value logic may carry over to metal, but their cost data do not. AM tools that never say whether they cover metal, including several spare-parts inventory models (Song and Zhang, Westerweel's lifecycle model, Cokyasar and Jin) and DoDI 5000.93, are kept and flagged "metal not stated" (62 of 215). The Navy's AMCAT counts as metal because NAVSEA, whose AM program is mostly metal, is wiring it into technical data package development. Ratings that public sources can't confirm are marked Unknown rather than No: 34% of all ratings, 43% for commercial products and 65% for government tools such as PACT, SEEKR and NIAR's TEA, which are known only from press releases or briefings.

Integration framework

Existing tools can be chained into a five-layer pipeline that passes each part's data, criticality class and costs forward; three missing modules would complete it. Every layer keys on the part number (NIIN), and the criticality class set at screening drives QA cost later. One-time costs (NRE, qualification, machines) stay separate from recurring costs, so the portfolio layer can spread them across parts.

  1. Part screening → ranked candidates, likely process, criticality class. Inputs: supply records (NIIN, demand, unit price, lead times, backorders, criticality code, technical data package status), drawings or CAD, and machine–material data. Tools: the DLA prescreener, 3YOURMIND or CASTOR, Coyle's Navy aviation screen, and NASA's AM-risk checklist. Missing: a standard map from supply criticality codes to QA classes, and a data-package flag that routes parts to NRE costing.
  2. Per-part cost → one-time NRE, plus unit cost and lead time by batch size. Inputs: candidates, machine rates, labor and post-processing steps. Tools: NIAR's planned four-process TEA model, the Braunberger and Crean models, Lindemann's activity-based model and AMPOWER, with NRE elements from Busachi. Missing: a reverse-engineering and data-package cost model; AFLCMC's $1M and Braunberger's 150 hours are only anchors.
  3. QA cost by criticality class → one-time qualification per machine–material pair, plus per-part inspection. Inputs: the class from ASTM F3572, NAVSEA severity levels or USAF structural categories. Tools: activity bundles from NASA-STD-6030, EZ-SB-19-01, NAVSEA tech pubs and MMPDS, priced with the Peron/Cantini structure and JMADD, Strutner and Verna cost data. Missing: priced bundles per class, and the cost of proving machine equivalence across sites (Delta Qual).
  4. Network and inventory model → storage savings, machines and sites, lead time under load, availability. Inputs: demand distributions, holding cost, backorder penalty, machine capacity, and the costs from layers 2 and 3. Tools: NCSU/USMA network models (Doudnikoff), Bonnín Roca's site model, Ghadge's and Sgarbossa's inventory comparisons, Peron's failure-rate model, and Jedeck's and O'Brien's shared-printer simulations. Missing: HIP, machining and inspection bottlenecks, per-machine qualification, and a dollar value per not-mission-capable day.
  5. Portfolio business case → which parts, processes, sites and qualifications to fund. Inputs: per-part results from layer 4, plus budgets. Tools: Busachi's decision support system, Braunberger's multi-objective scoring for non-dollar criteria, and NIAR's planned TEA module. Missing: an optimizer that spreads qualification and capital across services, with net present value and readiness outputs.

Feedback loops matter as much as the forward chain. Machine use from layer 4 changes unit cost in layer 2; build packing alone can shift it more than 2.5 times (Baumers et al.). The portfolio chosen in layer 5 sets how many parts share each qualified process, and so the per-part qualification cost in layer 3.

The three missing modules are an NRE cost model for legacy parts, priced QA bundles by criticality class, and a readiness value per not-mission-capable day. NIAR's TEA and CMU's Bonnín Roca model, which links process cost to site choice, are natural starting points (Bonnín Roca et al.).
